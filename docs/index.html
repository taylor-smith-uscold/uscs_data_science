00OrientationIntro

Data science at a cold storage 3PL

An internal guide to what our team does and the kinds of problems we can help with. It is written for people who know the business well and have no particular reason to know how modeling works.

The systems that run a cold storage operation record a great deal of what happens in a building. Receipts, picks, appointments, gate times, temperature readings, work orders, and billing transactions all leave a record, and most of that record is used once at the moment of the transaction and then sits in a database. Data science is the work of going back through it: answering questions that a single report cannot settle, and producing estimates about things that have not happened yet.

We wrote this guide because the most common reason a worthwhile project never starts is that the person closest to the problem did not realize it was the sort of thing to ask about. Sections 04 and 05 are aimed at that specifically.

Four kinds of question

Analytics work is conventionally sorted into four levels. The distinction is useful mainly because it indicates what sort of answer to expect and roughly how much work is involved in getting there.

LevelThe questionIn our business
DescriptiveWhat happened?What did the warehouse receive yesterday, by customer and zone?
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Why did dock dwell run high on Tuesdays last month?
PredictiveWhat is likely next?How many inbound pallets should we staff for next week?
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Which slots should we re-profile tonight, and in what order?

01FoundationsPrimer

What data science is

A working definition: using data, statistics, and software to answer questions that bear on a decision, and being explicit about how much confidence the answer deserves.

The field draws on three things at once. Statistics tells us whether a pattern in the data is real or the kind of variation that appears by chance. Engineering makes the data available in a workable form, on a schedule, without someone assembling it by hand each time. Domain knowledge determines whether the question is worth asking. Work that is short on any one of the three usually stalls, though the symptom differs depending on which one is missing.

The three pillars

Ⅰ

Statistics & modeling

Most operational numbers move around from week to week for no particular reason. Statistical methods estimate how much of an observed change is ordinary variation and how much is something new, and they attach a margin of error to the figures we report. Machine learning extends the same idea: rather than testing a relationship we already suspect, the method searches the historical record for one, and we then check whether it holds on data it was not fitted to.

Ⅱ

Data & software engineering

Putting a model to use requires a supply of data on a schedule. In practice that means reading from the WMS, TMS, ERP, refrigeration controls, and telemetry systems, reconciling customer and item identifiers that do not always agree between them, and placing the result somewhere a person will encounter it in the course of their work. In most projects this accounts for more of the effort than the modeling does.

Ⅲ

Domain knowledge

A blast freeze order and a dry-pick order look similar in a transaction table and behave nothing alike. Catch-weight items complicate every conversion between cases, pounds, and pallets. Lot traceability and FSMA requirements constrain what a recommendation is permitted to suggest. We depend on the people who work with these things daily to tell us when a result does not match what they observe.

Common misconceptions

A few points come up often enough in first conversations that they are worth stating directly.

What it does

  • Answers questions where the outcome depends on several factors at once, which is the usual reason a report has not settled the matter.
  • Produces estimates with a stated range: a most likely value of 1,240 pallets, with an upper end near 1,600.
  • Runs on a schedule once built, so the output does not depend on an analyst being at their desk.
  • Supports a decision. The person accountable for it still makes it.

What it does not do

  • Anticipate conditions with no precedent. Models are fitted to history, and a genuinely new situation falls outside what they have seen.
  • Correct errors in the source systems. Bad receipts or an out-of-date item master propagate into the output, often with no visible sign.
  • Replace reporting. Descriptive reporting answers what happened, and a good deal of the time that is the whole question.
  • Substitute for operational experience. It narrows the range a person has to judge between.

Before starting anything, we try to establish what would change if the answer were available and reliable. If the decision would be made the same way regardless, that is worth knowing at the outset rather than several weeks in.

02FoundationsMethod

How a project runs

Projects rarely proceed straight through. Reaching the modeling stage and finding something in the data that sends you back to the framing conversation is a normal outcome rather than a sign that something has gone wrong.

The seven steps

  1. Frame the decision

    Establish who makes the decision, how often it comes up, and what they would do differently with a better answer. We would rather learn at this stage that the answer would not change anything, since stopping here costs a conversation.

  2. Gather and understand the data

    Find the sources and learn how the fields are populated in practice, which is not always how they are documented. Gaps get written down rather than quietly worked around. This step generally takes longer than people expect, and shortening it tends to move a project's problems later rather than remove them.

  3. Prepare and explore

    Clean, join, and shape the data, then go through it with whoever knows the process. Where the numbers and the floor disagree, the disagreement is usually informative in its own right.

  4. Model

    Begin with a simple method and add complexity only where it measurably improves the result. Simpler models are also easier to explain, which bears directly on whether anyone ends up acting on the output.

  5. Validate

    Test on data the model has not seen, arranged the way the real decision is arranged. For a forecast, that means predicting a week using only information available before that week began. Measuring performance on the data a model was fitted to will overstate its accuracy, sometimes substantially.

  6. Deploy into the workflow

    Put the output where the decision happens: a report, an alert, or a screen in a system already in use. We try to settle this early, since it often constrains what the model can look like.

  7. Monitor and improve

    Track accuracy and use after release. Customers, SKUs, and sites change, and a model fitted to last year's conditions will gradually drift away from current ones. Retraining is part of operating the system.

Framing a project well

The technique matters less than the decision it feeds. As an illustration, “forecast inbound volume with a neural network” does not describe a project. It names a method with no stated user, timing, or purpose, and there is no way to tell whether it succeeded. “Give the shift planner a seven-day pallet forecast by Thursday afternoon, so labor can be scheduled against it” does describe one: it identifies who receives the output, when they need it, and what they will do with it. Requests that arrive in the second form are considerably easier to evaluate, and we can usually help turn one into the other during intake.

Typical timeline

StageTypical spanWhat comes out of it

03FoundationsModel

Four levels of analytics

The four levels describe what kind of question is being answered. The level a question sits at determines what data is required and how much work is involved.

The four levels

LevelQuestionWhat it involvesCold chain example
Descriptive What happened? Reporting and dashboards over recorded activity. Yesterday's receipts by customer and temperature zone.
Diagnostic Why did it happen? Drill-down, segmentation, and statistical testing to isolate what drove a number. Overtime rose because three customers shifted arrivals into the same window.
Predictive What is likely to happen? Models fitted to history estimate future values or probabilities, with a stated range. Expected inbound pallets per site per day over the next fourteen days.
Prescriptive What should we do about it? Optimization and simulation turn an estimate into a recommended action under real constraints. The dock-door and appointment plan that reduces trailer dwell tomorrow.

How the levels build on each other

Take overtime running high in a given week. Descriptive reporting shows that it happened. A diagnostic analysis attributes it to three customers' arrivals landing in the same window on Tuesday afternoons. A predictive model estimates that the same collision is likely again next Tuesday. A prescriptive system proposes which appointments to move and by how much, subject to carrier windows, door capacity, and the labor actually on shift.

Each level depends on the ones beneath it. Prescriptive optimization built on unreliable descriptive data will produce confident recommendations that are wrong, so the first weeks of a project are often spent on data quality rather than modeling. This is not the most visible kind of output, but it is frequently what the early phase of the work consists of.

Where the effort goes

The first two levels are largely a reporting and data engineering problem. The upper two are where modeling contributes most. A team that feels stuck at the descriptive level often has a data capture gap rather than a modeling gap, and of the two that is usually the cheaper to address.

04FoundationsTypes

Kinds of problems

Most requests we receive turn out to be one of a fairly small number of problem types. Recognizing which one you have indicates what data is needed and what form the answer will take.

The categories below overlap, and a real project often chains two or three of them together. A dock scheduling system, for example, estimates how long each load will take to service, which is regression, and then uses those estimates to build a door plan, which is optimization. The purpose of the list is not precise classification. It is to supply enough vocabulary that you can recognize when something you deal with regularly is a candidate for this kind of work.

Supervised: learning from recorded outcomes

These methods learn from examples where the answer was recorded. They require history in which the thing you want to predict was actually measured and stored. Without that record none of them are available, however much other data exists, which is why a feasibility check often comes down to whether outcomes were captured.

NumberSupervised

Regression

Estimates a numeric quantity from other things known at the time. The model is fitted on past cases where both the inputs and the resulting number were recorded.

Answers
How many, how much, how long.
Needs
Historical records where the value was measured, together with the conditions that preceded it.
Returns
A predicted number, normally with a range around it.
Here
Expected minutes to unload a load; cost to serve for a proposed account; expected pick time for an order profile.
Watch for
The range matters as much as the estimate. A prediction of 47 minutes that could plausibly fall between 20 and 90 is a different planning input than one that falls between 44 and 50.
CategorySupervised

Classification

Assigns a case to one of a set of categories, usually returning a probability for each rather than a hard label.

Answers
Will this happen? Which of these categories does it belong to?
Needs
History where the outcome category was recorded, with enough examples of the rarer category to learn from.
Returns
A probability, which becomes a yes or no once a threshold is chosen.
Here
Whether a load will miss its delivery window; whether an inventory adjustment is likely to be an error.
Watch for
Where the outcome is rare, overall accuracy is misleading, since predicting “no” every time can score well and be useless. Choosing the threshold is a business decision about the relative cost of a false alarm and a miss.
Dated seriesSupervised

Time-series forecasting

Projects a quantity forward over time, where the order of the observations carries information. Trend, seasonality, and day-of-week effects are modeled explicitly.

Answers
How much on each of the next N days or weeks?
Needs
A consistent history of the quantity over time, ideally covering several cycles of whatever seasonality matters.
Returns
A value per future period, with intervals that widen further out.
Here
Pallets received per site per day; case volume by customer through a seasonal peak.
Watch for
Known future events such as a promotion, a customer onboarding, or a plant shutdown have to be supplied as inputs. The model cannot infer them from history.
DurationSupervised

Time-to-event

Estimates how long until something occurs, while correctly handling the records for which it has not occurred yet. Also called survival analysis.

Answers
When is this likely to happen? What is the chance it happens within the next N months?
Needs
Records with a start date and either an event date or confirmation that the event had not occurred as of a known date.
Returns
A probability curve over time rather than a single date.
Here
Time until a piece of equipment needs unplanned service; tenure patterns across a hiring cohort.
Watch for
The treatment of records where the event has not happened yet is the whole point of the method. Dropping them, or treating them as if the event never occurs, biases the result in opposite directions.
Ordered listSupervised

Ranking

Orders a set of items by expected value or risk so that limited effort goes where it is worth most. Usually built on top of a regression or classification model.

Answers
Given that we can only get to twenty of these today, which twenty?
Needs
A per-item estimate of value or risk, and a realistic view of the capacity available to act on it.
Returns
An ordered worklist, ideally cut to the length that can actually be worked.
Here
Which locations to cycle count first; which accounts an account manager should contact this month.
Watch for
A list longer than the available capacity tends to be ignored altogether. Sizing the output to the work that can realistically be done is part of the design.

Unsupervised: finding structure with no recorded outcome

These methods do not need a recorded answer to learn from. They describe structure already present in the data. Because there is no ground truth to check against, results are judged on whether they are useful and whether they survive review by someone who knows the operation.

GroupsUnsupervised

Clustering

Groups records that resemble one another across many attributes at once, without being told in advance what the groups should be.

Answers
What natural groupings exist in this population?
Needs
A set of comparable attributes per record. No recorded outcome required.
Returns
A group assignment per record, and a profile of what makes each group distinct.
Here
SKU handling profiles as an input to slotting policy; customer segments defined by how accounts actually use the building rather than by size.
Watch for
There is no correct number of groups and no objective right answer. Clusters are worth keeping when they imply a different action for each group. Otherwise they are a description with nothing attached to it.
FlagsUnsupervised

Anomaly detection

Learns the normal range of behavior for a process or asset and flags departures from it. Useful where the failure modes are too varied or too rare to enumerate as fixed rules.

Answers
Is this reading, transaction, or pattern unusual for this asset or process?
Needs
Enough history of normal operation. Recorded examples of failures help but are not strictly required.
Returns
A flag with a score indicating how far the observation sits from normal.
Here
A compressor drawing more power than its own history would predict; unusual adjustment patterns at a location.
Watch for
Unusual is not the same as wrong. Every anomaly system needs a route for someone to confirm or dismiss a flag, and that feedback is what keeps the alert volume manageable.
Co-occurrenceUnsupervised

Affinity

Identifies items or events that occur together more often than chance alone would explain.

Answers
What tends to appear alongside what?
Needs
Transaction records that group items into baskets, orders, or events.
Returns
Pairs or sets, with measures of how strong and how frequent the association is.
Here
SKUs frequently picked on the same order, which feeds slotting; alarm codes that tend to precede a particular failure.
Watch for
Co-occurrence can reflect a shared cause rather than a relationship worth acting on, and sheer volume will make popular items appear associated with nearly everything.
Composite indicesUnsupervised

Dimensionality reduction

Condenses many correlated measurements into a few composite figures that retain most of the variation in the original set.

Answers
Can these forty sensor channels be summarized into something a person can monitor?
Needs
Many numeric attributes measured consistently across records.
Returns
A small number of derived indices, with a description of what each one captures.
Here
A health index per refrigeration asset built from many telemetry channels; condensing a wide activity profile before clustering it.
Watch for
The derived indices are combinations of the originals, and their interpretation is not always obvious. This is more often a preparation step than a deliverable in itself.

Prescriptive: choosing an action

Prediction estimates what will happen. These methods take the further step of recommending what to do about it, given the constraints the operation actually works under.

A planPrescriptive

Optimization

Searches for the best available plan subject to stated constraints, in situations where the number of possible plans is far too large to evaluate by hand.

Answers
Given our costs, capacity, and rules, what is the best assignment or schedule?
Needs
Constraints written down explicitly, an objective people agree on, and reliable input data.
Returns
A specific recommended plan, usually with the cost difference against the current one.
Here
Door and appointment assignment; load building and routing; a re-slotting move list.
Watch for
Writing down the constraints is normally the hard part. Rules that are followed on the floor but never documented will be violated by the solver, and the recommendation will be dismissed for good reason.
Scenario outcomesPrescriptive

Simulation

Represents the operation as a system and runs it many times under varied conditions, so that a proposed change can be examined before it is committed to.

Answers
What would happen if we changed this? How bad is the worst plausible case?
Needs
A description of the process, distributions for the variable parts, and historical data to calibrate against.
Returns
A distribution of outcomes per scenario rather than a single figure.
Here
The effect on dwell of adding two doors or changing the appointment grid; staffing needed to absorb a seasonal peak.
Watch for
A simulation is only as good as its assumptions and will produce plausible-looking output whether or not they hold. Reproducing a period that already happened is the usual check.

Causal: attributing a change

Prediction and causation are different questions, and a method that is good at one is not automatically good at the other. A model can forecast overtime accurately from variables that do not cause it. When the question is whether something worked, the analysis has to be designed for that from the start.

Effect estimateCausal

Causal inference and experiment design

Estimates the effect of a change, separated from everything else that moved at the same time. Where a controlled comparison can be arranged in advance that is the stronger option; where it cannot, several methods approximate one after the fact.

Answers
Did this change cause the improvement, and how large was the effect?
Needs
Some basis for comparison, such as sites, shifts, or periods that did not receive the change, along with measurements from before and after.
Returns
An estimated effect size with an interval, and an explicit statement of the assumptions it rests on.
Here
Whether a revised pick path raised cases per hour or whether volume mix explains it; whether a rate change affected volume.
Watch for
Before-and-after comparisons with no control group are the most common way a modest effect gets reported as a large one. Deciding how the comparison will be made before a change is rolled out costs very little and improves the answer considerably.

Unstructured: working with text

Structured fieldsText

Text mining and extraction

Turns free-text records into fields that can be counted, grouped, and joined to other data.

Answers
What are people actually recording in these notes, and how often?
Needs
A body of text records, and agreement on which categories or fields would be useful.
Returns
Categories, extracted values, or summaries attached to each record.
Here
Categorizing claims and complaints by cause; pulling equipment and fault details out of maintenance notes.
Watch for
Free text tends to be inconsistent, abbreviated, and specific to a site or a person. Some manual labeling is nearly always needed to set the categories and check the output, and text on its own rarely settles a question without structured data alongside it.

Where to start

You do not need to identify the type before contacting us. Requests that arrive described as one type frequently turn out to be another, and some are answered adequately by a report or a query. Describing the decision, as covered in section 06, is enough to begin with.

06Working togetherProcess

Bringing us a problem

Working out which method applies is our job. What we need from you is an account of the decision and what makes it difficult. Given that, we can usually say within a week or so whether the problem is tractable.

A good problem statement

The decision
What is being decided, by whom, and how often it comes up.
Today's method
How it is decided now: a report, experience, a spreadsheet someone maintains.
Cost of being wrong
What the consequences are when the current approach misses. Overtime, detention, spoilage, a lost account.
Timing
When the answer would need to arrive to be usable. A forecast that lands after the schedule is published cannot be acted on.
Available data
Which systems are involved in the process. A rough list is enough; tracking down the detail is part of our work.
Success
What you would expect to see change if it worked.

What makes a good candidate

Some problems suit this kind of work better than others. The distinction is mostly about whether there is a record to learn from and a decision that can absorb the answer.

Ready

  • The decision recurs: daily, weekly, per load, per SKU.
  • The outcome is recorded somewhere, so there is history to learn from.
  • Several factors interact, which is usually why a report has not settled it.
  • Someone owns the decision and is prepared to change how they make it.

Not yet

  • The process is undocumented, or varies between sites with no record of the differences.
  • The relevant data exists only on paper or in free-text notes.
  • The question is one-off. A direct analysis will answer it sooner than a model would.
  • The obstacle is a policy or system constraint rather than a lack of information.

Items in the second column are not refusals. Several of them describe work we can help with directly, and the data capture and documentation involved often has value independent of whatever gets built afterwards.

Glossary

Terms that come up in a project kickoff, in plain language.

Model

A rule fitted to historical data that turns inputs into an output. For example, taking customer, day of week, season, and appointment count, and returning an expected number of pallets received.

Feature

An input to a model. Day of week, ambient temperature, SKU velocity, and customer tenure are all features. Deciding which ones to include draws heavily on knowledge of the operation.

Label, or target

The value a supervised model is trained to reproduce, meaning the recorded answer. Whether a usable label exists in the historical data usually determines whether a supervised approach is possible at all.

Supervised and unsupervised learning

Supervised methods learn from examples where the outcome was recorded. Unsupervised methods describe structure in data with no recorded outcome, such as groupings or unusual observations. The distinction determines what data a project needs before it can start.

Training and validation

Training is fitting the model to past data. Validation is measuring its performance on data held back from that process, which gives a more realistic estimate of how it will behave on decisions that have not been made yet.

Machine learning

A set of methods that fit patterns from data rather than following rules written by hand. It is one part of data science rather than another name for it.

Prediction interval

The range around a forecast. A point estimate of 1,240 pallets says less than an estimate of 1,240 with a range of roughly 1,050 to 1,480, which indicates how much slack a plan needs.

Constraint

A rule an optimization result has to respect: door capacity, carrier windows, temperature compatibility, stacking limits. Constraints that are followed in practice but never written down are a common reason a recommendation looks wrong to the people receiving it.

Optimization

Selecting the best option available under a set of constraints, such as the lowest-cost load plan that still meets delivery windows, temperature requirements, and equipment limits.

Anomaly detection

Establishing what normal behavior looks like for a process or asset, so that departures from it can be flagged without someone specifying a threshold in advance.

Causal inference

Methods for estimating whether a change caused an observed result rather than merely preceding it. These require some form of comparison, which is far easier to arrange before a change is rolled out than afterwards.

Model drift

The gradual decline in a model's accuracy as conditions change. New customers, SKUs, and sites all shift the patterns a model was fitted to, so periodic retraining is part of operating it.

Data pipeline

The automated path data takes from source systems into a form a model can use. A large share of the work in most projects sits here, and it is what allows a result to be reproduced rather than assembled once by hand.

Ground truth

The recorded, verified outcome that predictions are compared against. Without it a model cannot be trained or scored, which is why capturing outcomes is worth doing even before a project exists.

If a term comes up that is not here, let us know and it will be added.
