00OrientationIntro

Turning cold chain data into decisions you can act on

Every pallet we receive, every door we open, every degree we hold, and every mile we drive leaves a data trail. Data science is the discipline of turning that trail into forecasts, recommendations, and automated decisions — so operators, planners, and commercial teams spend less time reacting and more time getting ahead.

This guide is written for internal users who are not data specialists. It assumes you know the business cold and the modeling not at all, which is the right way round: the hardest part of any project is knowing which question matters, and that knowledge lives on the floor, in the yard, and in the account reviews — not in the model.

Four questions, four levels of leverage

Analytics work climbs a ladder. Each rung answers a different kind of question, and each one is worth more than the one below it because it moves closer to the decision itself.

LevelThe questionIn our business
DescriptiveWhat happened?What did the warehouse receive yesterday, by customer and zone?
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Why did dock dwell spike on Tuesdays last month?
PredictiveWhat is likely next?How many inbound pallets should we staff for next week?
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Which slots should we re-profile tonight, in what order?

01FoundationsPrimer

What data science is

The practice of using data, statistics, and software to answer questions and make better decisions — and, just as importantly, to say how confident we are in the answer.

Data science sits where three things overlap: the mathematics that tells you whether a pattern is real, the engineering that makes data usable at scale, and the domain knowledge that tells you which questions are worth asking in the first place. Miss any one of the three and you get something that looks impressive in a slide deck but changes nothing about what anyone does on Monday morning.

The three pillars

Ⅰ

Statistics & modeling

Separating signal from noise. Is throughput really down, or is this ordinary week-to-week variation? Statistics quantifies uncertainty so we don't chase randomness. Machine learning extends it — learning patterns from history in order to score or forecast situations we haven't seen yet.

Ⅱ

Data & software engineering

A model is worthless if it can't be fed. This is the plumbing: pulling from the WMS, TMS, ERP, telemetry, and refrigeration controls; reconciling customer and item master data; and delivering results into a dashboard, an alert, or the system an operator already has open.

Ⅲ

Domain knowledge

Knowing that a blast freeze order behaves nothing like a dry-pick order, that catch-weight items complicate every unit conversion, and that FSMA and lot traceability are non-negotiable. The domain expert in the room is usually you — which is why these projects are partnerships, not deliveries.

What it is, and what it isn't

Expectations do more damage than models do. It is worth being blunt about both sides.

It is

  • A way to answer questions a single report can't — because the answer depends on many interacting factors.
  • Quantified uncertainty: most likely 1,240 pallets, and rarely above 1,600.
  • A repeatable process, versioned and monitored, that runs whether or not an analyst is at their desk.
  • Decision support. The judgment stays with the operator, planner, or account manager.

It isn't

  • A crystal ball. Models learn from history; genuinely new conditions still require human judgment.
  • A substitute for clean data. Bad receipts and a stale item master produce confident nonsense.
  • Just dashboards. Reporting tells you what happened; data science tells you what is likely next and what to do about it.
  • A replacement for the people doing the work. It removes guesswork, not expertise.

The useful test is not “is this sophisticated?” but “if this were right, would anyone do anything differently?” A model nobody acts on is an expensive opinion.

02FoundationsMethod

How a data science project actually runs

Projects follow a loop, not a straight line. It is normal — and healthy — to reach step four, learn something surprising, and go back to step one with a sharper question.

The seven steps

  1. Frame the decision

    Who decides what, how often, and what would they do differently with a better answer? If nothing changes, the project stops here — on purpose, and early, while it is cheap to stop.

  2. Gather & understand the data

    Locate the sources, learn how the fields are really populated in practice rather than in the documentation, and write down the gaps. This is usually the longest step, and skipping it is the most common way a project fails late instead of early.

  3. Prepare & explore

    Clean, join, and shape the data. Explore it alongside the business expert to confirm that what the numbers say matches what the floor sees. Disagreements here are findings, not annoyances.

  4. Model

    Start with the simplest thing that could work, then earn complexity. A well-tuned baseline that people trust beats an elaborate model that nobody opens.

  5. Validate

    Test on data the model has never seen, arranged in the same shape as the real decision — for example, forecasting next week using only what we would have known last week. Anything else flatters the model.

  6. Deploy into the workflow

    Deliver the output where the decision is made: a report, an alert, a screen in an existing system. Adoption is a design problem, not an afterthought.

  7. Monitor & improve

    Track accuracy and usage over time. Business conditions drift — new customers, new SKUs, new sites — and models must be retrained to keep up.

A rule of thumb

A good project is defined by the decision it improves, not the technique it uses.

“Forecast inbound volume with a neural network” is a solution looking for a problem. “Give the shift planner a seven-day pallet forecast by 3 p.m. Thursday, so labor can be scheduled with confidence” is a project: it names the user, the timing, the output, and the decision it feeds.

What the calendar looks like

StageTypical spanWhat comes out of it
IntakeWeek 0The problem restated as a decision, and a shared view of whether it is worth solving
FeasibilityWeeks 1–2An honest go / no-go: does the data exist, and is there signal in it?
PrototypeWeeks 3–8A working model reviewed with the business expert and tested against history
PilotOngoingOne site running it for real, with accuracy and adoption tracked
ProductionOngoingAutomated refresh, monitoring, retraining, and expansion where it holds up

Spans are indicative. A well-scoped forecasting problem on clean WMS data moves faster than an optimization problem that first needs a new data capture step on the floor.

03FoundationsModel

Four levels of analytics

Each level answers a different question and delivers more leverage than the last. Most teams already live on the first two rungs; data science is how you climb to the third and fourth.

The ladder

LevelQuestionWhat it involvesCold chain example
Descriptive What happened? Standard reporting and dashboards over recorded activity. Yesterday's receipts by customer and temperature zone.
Diagnostic Why did it happen? Drill-down, segmentation, and statistical testing to isolate the drivers behind a number. Overtime rose because three customers shifted arrivals into the same window.
Predictive What is likely to happen? Models trained on history estimate future values or probabilities, with a stated confidence range. Expected inbound pallets per site per day for the next fourteen days.
Prescriptive What should we do about it? Optimization and simulation turn a prediction into a recommended action under real constraints. The dock-door and appointment plan that minimizes trailer dwell tomorrow.

Why the climb matters

A descriptive report tells a supervisor that overtime ran high last week. A diagnostic analysis tells them it happened because three customers' arrivals collided on Tuesday afternoons. A predictive model tells them it is going to happen again next Tuesday. A prescriptive system tells them which appointments to move, and by how much, to prevent it — while respecting carrier windows, door capacity, and the labor actually on shift.

Each rung is built on the one below. There is no skipping to prescriptive optimization in a process where the descriptive data is unreliable, which is why early projects often deliver unglamorous data quality work as their first real output.

Where the effort goes

In practice the first two rungs are largely a reporting and data engineering problem, and the upper two are where modeling earns its keep. Teams that feel stuck on rung one usually have a data capture gap, not a modeling gap — and that is a much cheaper problem to fix.

04Applied15 cases

Where it pays off in a cold storage 3PL

Illustrative of the problem shapes data science handles well in our industry. Each one names the pain, the data it draws on, the approach, and what an internal user actually receives.

PredictiveWarehouse ops

Inbound & outbound volume forecasting

Shift plans are built on last week's actuals and a phone call. When a customer's promotion or a seasonal pack runs hot, the floor finds out at the guard shack.

Data
WMS receipt and order history, appointment schedule, customer forecasts, calendar and seasonality, promotional and holiday flags
Approach
Time-series forecasting per site and customer, with prediction intervals and a bottom-up roll-up to the network
Output
A fourteen-day pallet and case forecast by site, refreshed daily, flagging days expected to exceed comfortable capacity
PrescriptiveWarehouse ops

Slotting & storage optimization

Fast movers end up at the back of the freezer, selectors walk further than they should, and re-slotting happens only once someone notices it has gotten bad.

Data
Pick history by SKU, location master and travel distances, pallet dimensions and weight, temperature zone constraints, lot and expiry rules
Approach
Velocity and affinity analysis feeding a constrained optimization that respects zone, stacking, and FEFO rules
Output
A ranked weekly re-slotting worklist sized to what a shift can absorb, with estimated travel saved per move
PrescriptiveWarehouse ops

Dock scheduling & trailer dwell

Appointments cluster in the morning, doors sit idle in the afternoon, and detention charges follow. Nobody sees the whole day's shape until it is already happening.

Data
Appointment and gate timestamps, door assignment history, unload and load durations by load type, yard moves, labor availability
Approach
Predicted service time per load feeding a scheduling optimizer that levels arrivals across doors and hours
Output
A recommended appointment grid and door plan, plus same-day alerts when the schedule drifts toward a bottleneck
PredictiveLabor

Labor demand planning & overtime control

Staffing decisions are made days ahead, but volume lands hourly. The gap is absorbed by overtime on heavy days and idle time on light ones.

Data
Volume forecast, labor standards by activity, timeclock and attendance history, shift patterns, training and certification records
Approach
Convert forecast workload into required hours by function and hour of day, then match against the available roster
Output
A staffing recommendation by shift and function, showing projected overtime and understaffing risk side by side
PredictiveLabor

Turnover & retention risk

Freezer environments are demanding, and losing a trained selector costs far more than the open shift it creates. HR usually learns about a resignation after the decision is made.

Data
Tenure, shift and schedule stability, overtime load, absence patterns, pay progression, site and role — aggregated and privacy-reviewed
Approach
Cohort survival analysis and classification models identifying which conditions precede voluntary departures
Output
Site-level drivers of turnover ranked by impact, so intervention targets scheduling and workload rather than guesswork
PrescriptiveTransportation

Route, mode & consolidation planning

LTL shipments leave on separate trucks to neighboring destinations because the consolidation opportunity wasn't visible when the load was tendered.

Data
TMS order and shipment history, lane rates and accessorials, carrier performance, delivery windows, equipment and temperature requirements
Approach
Load-building and vehicle-routing optimization with temperature compatibility and appointment windows as hard constraints
Output
Suggested consolidations and mode changes with an estimated cost delta per option, surfaced before tender
PredictiveTransportation

Arrival-time & on-time delivery prediction

A carrier says they'll be there at eight. The site plans for eight. Reality is a distribution, not a promise, and the schedule absorbs the difference.

Data
Historical gate-in and gate-out times, carrier and lane performance, distance and traffic patterns, weather, load type
Approach
Regression and classification models producing a probability-weighted arrival window per load
Output
A live risk board of loads unlikely to make their window, early enough to reshuffle doors or notify the customer
PrescriptiveEnergy

Refrigeration energy & peak demand

Refrigeration is one of the largest controllable costs in the building, and demand charges are set by a handful of peak intervals nobody saw coming.

Data
Interval meter data, utility tariff structure, compressor and setpoint logs, room temperatures, ambient weather, door-open events, product load
Approach
Consumption modeling plus optimization of pre-cooling and load-shifting within safe temperature bands
Output
Day-ahead load-shift recommendations, peak-risk alerts, and site benchmarking normalized for weather and occupancy
PredictiveMaintenance

Predictive maintenance for critical assets

A compressor or dock leveler fails mid-shift. The repair is the small cost; the product at risk and the disrupted schedule are the large ones.

Data
Equipment telemetry and alarm history, run hours, work orders and parts usage, ambient conditions, asset age and make
Approach
Anomaly detection on sensor patterns and failure-probability models scored on a rolling window
Output
A prioritized maintenance queue flagging assets drifting out of their own normal behavior before they trip an alarm
DiagnosticInventory

Inventory accuracy & shrink detection

Cycle counts catch discrepancies late, and root cause is reconstructed from memory weeks after the transaction that caused it.

Data
Transaction-level WMS movements, adjustment history, cycle count results, license plate and pallet genealogy, catch-weight variances
Approach
Anomaly detection across transaction patterns, with counting effort targeted where error probability is highest
Output
A risk-ranked cycle count list instead of a round-robin, plus early flags on locations and processes generating variance
PredictiveQuality & food safety

Temperature excursion & shelf-life risk

Excursions are reviewed after the fact. By the time a report surfaces the event, the product has moved and the customer conversation is reactive.

Data
Room and trailer temperature telemetry, door-open events, receipt and ship timestamps, product tolerance specs, lot and expiry data
Approach
Streaming anomaly detection on temperature curves, combined with cumulative time-temperature exposure scoring by lot
Output
Real-time excursion alerts routed to the right person, and a lot-level risk view supporting FEFO and traceability decisions
PredictiveSpace planning

Space & capacity planning

Occupancy is managed to a monthly number, but the crunch happens on specific weeks — and overselling space is far more expensive than knowing about it early.

Data
Pallet position inventory over time, contracted versus actual usage by customer, inbound forecast, dwell-time distributions by SKU
Approach
Forecast in-and-out flows to project occupancy forward, with simulation of customer-mix scenarios
Output
A projected occupancy curve by site with at-risk weeks highlighted, giving sales and ops time to place volume elsewhere
DiagnosticFinance

Customer & SKU profitability

Revenue is visible per customer; true cost to serve is not. Handling-heavy, low-storage accounts can look identical to their opposite on a P&L summary.

Data
Billing and rate tables, activity-level transactions (receipts, picks, loads, blast, VAS), labor minutes by activity, space occupied over time
Approach
Activity-based costing driven by measured effort, then margin decomposition by customer, site, and SKU profile
Output
A margin waterfall per account showing which activities consume it — the evidence base for renewal and rate conversations
PredictiveCommercial

Churn & volume-decline early warning

Accounts rarely leave suddenly. Volume tapers, order patterns change, and the signals are spread across months of transactions nobody reads end to end.

Data
Volume and order-frequency trends, service performance history, claims and complaints, contract terms and renewal dates
Approach
Change-point detection on account trajectories plus a classification model for attrition risk
Output
A monthly watch list for account managers ordered by revenue at risk, with the specific signals that triggered each flag
PrescriptivePricing

Quote & rate decision support

New business is priced from comparable deals and instinct. Two accounts with similar pallet counts can impose very different demands on the building.

Data
Historical quotes and outcomes, realized cost to serve by account profile, site capacity and constraints, seasonality of the incoming volume
Approach
Predicted cost to serve for the proposed profile, combined with margin scenarios across a rate range
Output
A pre-quote profitability estimate with sensitivity to volume and mix assumptions, produced in minutes rather than days

05Working togetherProcess

Bringing us a problem

You do not need to know what model to use, or whether the data exists. You need to know what decision is hard. Bring the problem in this shape and we can usually tell you within a week whether it is tractable.

A good problem statement

The decision
What is being decided, by whom, and how often?
Today's method
How is it decided now — a report, experience, a spreadsheet someone maintains?
Cost of being wrong
What happens when the current approach misses? Overtime, detention, spoilage, a lost account?
Timing
When would the answer need to arrive to be useful? An accurate forecast that lands after the schedule is published is worth nothing.
Available data
Which systems touch this process? A rough list is enough — we will do the archaeology.
Success
What would you expect to see change if this worked?

What makes a good candidate

Ready

  • The decision repeats — daily, weekly, per load, per SKU.
  • The outcome is recorded somewhere, so we can learn from history.
  • Several factors interact, which is why a single report hasn't solved it.
  • Someone owns the decision and is willing to change how they make it.

Not yet

  • The underlying process isn't documented, or varies by site with no record of the difference.
  • The data is captured on paper or in free-text notes only.
  • It's a one-time question — a good analysis answers it faster than a model would.
  • The real blocker is a policy or system change, not a lack of insight.

“Not yet” is not a rejection. Several of the items above are themselves small projects, and the work to fix them tends to pay for itself before any model is built.

06ReferenceRef

Plain-language glossary

The vocabulary you will hear in a project kickoff, without the jargon tax.

Model

A rule learned from historical data that turns inputs into an output — for example, turning “customer, day of week, season, appointment count” into “expected pallets received.”

Feature

An input to a model. Day of week, ambient temperature, SKU velocity, and customer tenure are all features. Choosing good features is where domain knowledge pays off most.

Training & validation

Training is the model learning from past data. Validation is testing it on data it never saw, to estimate how it will perform on next week's real decisions rather than on the past it memorized.

Machine learning

A family of methods that learn patterns from data instead of being programmed with explicit rules. It is a subset of data science, not a synonym for it.

Prediction interval

The honest range around a forecast. “1,240 pallets” is a guess; “most likely 1,240, and ninety percent of the time between 1,050 and 1,480” is a plan you can staff to.

Optimization

Finding the best option under constraints — the lowest-cost load plan that still honors delivery windows, temperature requirements, and equipment limits.

Anomaly detection

Learning what “normal” looks like so unusual behavior — a compressor drawing more power than its own history predicts — surfaces automatically rather than waiting for a threshold alarm.

Model drift

Performance decaying as the business changes. New customers, new SKUs, and new sites all shift the patterns a model learned, which is why monitoring and retraining are part of the deliverable.

Data pipeline

The automated path data takes from source systems to a form the model can use. Most of the effort in a project lives here, and it is what makes results repeatable instead of one-off.

Ground truth

The recorded, verified outcome we compare predictions against. Without it a model can't be trained or scored — which is why capturing outcomes is worth the effort even before a project starts.
