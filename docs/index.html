Data science is the discipline of turning that trail into forecasts, recommendations, and automated decisions — so operators, planners, and commercial teams spend less time reacting and more time getting ahead.

01 — Foundations

What is data science?

Data science is the practice of using data, statistics, and software to answer questions and make better decisions. It sits where three things overlap: the math that tells you whether a pattern is real, the engineering that makes data usable at scale, and the domain knowledge that tells you which questions are worth asking in the first place. Miss any one of the three and you get something that looks impressive but doesn't change what anyone does on Monday morning.

Ⅰ

Statistics & modeling

Separating signal from noise. Is throughput really down, or is this ordinary week-to-week variation? Statistics quantifies uncertainty so we don't chase randomness, and machine learning extends it — learning patterns from history to score or forecast situations we haven't seen yet.

Ⅱ

Data & software engineering

A model is worthless if it can't be fed. This is the plumbing: pulling from the WMS, TMS, ERP, telemetry, and refrigeration controls; reconciling customer and item master data; and delivering results into a dashboard, an alert, or the system an operator already uses.

Ⅲ

Domain knowledge

Knowing that a blast freeze order behaves nothing like a dry-pick order, that catch-weight items complicate every unit conversion, and that FSMA and lot traceability are non-negotiable. The domain expert in the room is usually you — which is why these projects are partnerships.

What data science is

  • A way to answer questions that a single report can't — because the answer depends on many interacting factors.
  • Quantified uncertainty: "most likely 1,240 pallets, and rarely above 1,600."
  • A repeatable process, versioned and monitored, that runs whether or not an analyst is at their desk.
  • A decision-support tool. The judgment stays with the operator, planner, or account manager.

What it isn't

  • Not a crystal ball. Models learn from history; genuinely new conditions require human judgment.
  • Not a substitute for clean data. Bad receipts and stale item masters produce confident nonsense.
  • Not just dashboards. Reporting tells you what happened; data science tells you what's likely next and what to do.
  • Not a replacement for the people doing the work. It removes guesswork, not expertise.

02 — Method

How a data science project actually runs

Projects follow a loop, not a straight line. It is normal — and healthy — to reach step four, learn something surprising, and go back to step one with a sharper question.

  1. 1

    Frame the decision

    Who decides what, how often, and what would they do differently with a better answer? If nothing changes, the project stops here — on purpose.

  2. 2

    Gather & understand the data

    Locate the sources, learn how the fields are really populated in practice, and document the gaps. This is usually the longest step.

  3. 3

    Prepare & explore

    Clean, join, and shape the data. Explore it with the business expert to confirm that what the numbers say matches what the floor sees.

  4. 4

    Model

    Start with the simplest thing that could work, then earn complexity. A well-tuned baseline beats an elaborate model nobody trusts.

  5. 5

    Validate

    Test on data the model has never seen, in the same shape as the real decision — for example, forecasting next week using only what we'd have known last week.

  6. 6

    Deploy into the workflow

    Deliver the output where the decision is made: a report, an alert, a screen in an existing system. Adoption is a design problem, not an afterthought.

  7. 7

    Monitor & improve

    Track accuracy and usage over time. Business conditions drift — new customers, new SKUs, new sites — and models must be retrained to keep up.

Rule of thumb: a good project is defined by the decision it improves, not the technique it uses. "Forecast inbound volume with a neural network" is a solution looking for a problem. "Give the shift planner a seven-day pallet forecast by 3 p.m. Thursday so labor can be scheduled with confidence" is a project.

03 — The ladder

Four levels of analytics

Each level answers a different question and delivers more leverage than the last. Most teams already live on the first two rungs; data science is how you climb to the third and fourth.

Level 1

Descriptive

“What happened?”

Throughput, occupancy, cases picked per hour, on-time percentage. Standard reporting and dashboards.

Cold chain example Yesterday's receipts by customer and temperature zone.

Level 2

Diagnostic

“Why did it happen?”

Drill-down, segmentation, and statistical testing to isolate the drivers behind a number.

Cold chain example Overtime rose because three customers shifted arrivals into the same window.

Level 3

Predictive

“What is likely to happen?”

Models trained on history estimate future values or probabilities, with a stated confidence range.

Cold chain example Expected inbound pallets per site per day for the next 14 days.

Level 4

Prescriptive

“What should we do about it?”

Optimization and simulation turn a prediction into a recommended action under real constraints.

Cold chain example The specific dock-door and appointment plan that minimizes trailer dwell tomorrow.

04 — Applied

Where it pays off in a cold storage 3PL

The examples below are illustrative of the problem shapes data science handles well in our industry. Each one names the pain, the data it draws on, the approach, and what an internal user actually receives. Filter by the team you work with.

Predictive Warehouse Ops

Inbound & outbound volume forecasting

Shift plans are built on last week's actuals and a phone call. When a customer's promotion or a seasonal pack runs hot, the floor finds out at the guard shack.

Data
WMS receipt & order history, appointment schedule, customer forecasts, calendar and seasonality, promotional and holiday flags
Approach
Time-series forecasting per site & customer, with prediction intervals and a bottom-up roll-up to the network
You get
A 14-day pallet and case forecast by site, refreshed daily, with a flagged list of days expected to exceed comfortable capacity
Prescriptive Warehouse Ops

Slotting & storage optimization

Fast movers end up in the back of the freezer, selectors walk further than they should, and re-slotting happens only when someone notices the problem has gotten bad.

Data
Pick history by SKU, location master & travel distances, pallet dimensions and weight, temperature zone constraints, lot/expiry rules
Approach
Velocity and affinity analysis feeding a constrained optimization that respects zone, stacking, and FEFO rules
You get
A ranked, weekly re-slotting worklist sized to the moves a shift can realistically absorb — with estimated travel saved per move
Prescriptive Warehouse Ops

Dock scheduling & trailer dwell reduction

Appointments cluster in the morning, doors sit idle in the afternoon, and detention charges follow. Nobody can see the whole day's shape until it's already happening.

Data
Appointment and gate timestamps, door assignment history, unload/load durations by load type, yard moves, labor availability
Approach
Predicted service time per load feeding a scheduling optimizer that levels arrivals across doors and hours
You get
A recommended appointment grid and door plan, plus same-day alerts when the schedule drifts toward a bottleneck
Predictive Labor

Labor demand planning & overtime control

Staffing decisions are made days ahead, but volume lands hourly. The gap is absorbed by overtime on heavy days and idle time on light ones.

Data
Volume forecast, labor standards by activity, timeclock and attendance history, shift patterns, training/certification records
Approach
Convert forecast workload into required hours by function and hour of day, then match against the available roster
You get
A staffing recommendation by shift and function with the projected overtime and understaffing risk shown side by side
Predictive Labor

Turnover & retention risk

Freezer environments are demanding, and losing a trained selector costs far more than the open shift it creates. HR usually learns about a resignation after the decision is made.

Data
Tenure, shift and schedule stability, overtime load, absence patterns, pay progression, site and role (aggregated, privacy-reviewed)
Approach
Cohort survival analysis and classification models identifying which conditions precede voluntary departures
You get
Site-level drivers of turnover ranked by impact, so intervention targets scheduling and workload — not guesswork
Prescriptive Transportation

Route, mode & consolidation planning

LTL shipments leave on separate trucks to neighboring destinations because the consolidation opportunity wasn't visible when the load was tendered.

Data
TMS order and shipment history, lane rates and accessorials, carrier performance, delivery windows, equipment and temperature requirements
Approach
Load-building and vehicle-routing optimization with temperature compatibility and appointment windows as hard constraints
You get
Suggested consolidations and mode changes with the estimated cost delta per option, surfaced before tender
Predictive Transportation

Arrival-time & on-time delivery prediction

A carrier says they'll be there at 8. The site plans for 8. Reality is a distribution, not a promise, and the schedule absorbs the difference.

Data
Historical gate-in/gate-out times, carrier and lane performance, distance and traffic patterns, weather, load type
Approach
Regression and classification models producing a probability-weighted arrival window per load
You get
A live risk board of loads unlikely to make their window, early enough to reshuffle doors or notify the customer
Prescriptive Energy

Refrigeration energy & peak demand management

Refrigeration is one of the largest controllable costs in the building, and demand charges are set by a handful of peak intervals nobody saw coming.

Data
Interval meter data, utility tariff structure, compressor and setpoint logs, room temperatures, ambient weather, door-open events, product load
Approach
Consumption modeling plus optimization of pre-cooling and load-shifting within safe temperature bands
You get
Day-ahead load-shift recommendations, peak-risk alerts, and site-by-site benchmarking that normalizes for weather and occupancy
Predictive Maintenance

Predictive maintenance for critical assets

A compressor or dock leveler fails mid-shift. The repair is the small cost; the product at risk and the disrupted schedule are the large ones.

Data
Equipment telemetry and alarm history, run hours, work orders and parts usage, ambient conditions, asset age and make
Approach
Anomaly detection on sensor patterns and failure-probability models scored on a rolling window
You get
A prioritized maintenance queue that flags assets drifting out of normal behavior before they trip an alarm
Diagnostic Inventory

Inventory accuracy & shrink detection

Cycle counts catch discrepancies late, and root cause is reconstructed from memory weeks after the transaction that caused it.

Data
Transaction-level WMS movements, adjustment history, cycle count results, license plate/pallet genealogy, catch-weight variances
Approach
Anomaly detection across transaction patterns, with counting effort targeted where error probability is highest
You get
A risk-ranked cycle count list instead of a round-robin, plus early flags on locations and processes generating variance
Predictive Quality & Food Safety

Temperature excursion & shelf-life risk

Excursions are reviewed after the fact. By the time a report surfaces the event, the product has moved and the customer conversation is reactive.

Data
Room and trailer temperature telemetry, door-open events, receipt and ship timestamps, product tolerance specs, lot and expiry data
Approach
Streaming anomaly detection on temperature curves combined with cumulative time-temperature exposure scoring by lot
You get
Real-time excursion alerts routed to the right person, and a lot-level risk view that supports FEFO and traceability decisions
Predictive Space Planning

Space & capacity planning

Occupancy is managed to a monthly number, but the crunch happens on specific weeks — and overselling space is far more expensive than knowing about it early.

Data
Pallet position inventory over time, contracted vs. actual usage by customer, inbound forecast, dwell-time distributions by SKU
Approach
Forecast in-and-out flows to project occupancy forward, with simulation of customer-mix scenarios
You get
A projected occupancy curve by site with weeks at risk highlighted, giving sales and ops time to place volume elsewhere
Diagnostic Finance

Customer & SKU profitability

Revenue is visible per customer; true cost to serve is not. Handling-heavy, low-storage accounts can look identical to their opposite on a P&L summary.

Data
Billing and rate tables, activity-level transactions (receipts, picks, loads, blast, VAS), labor minutes by activity, space occupied over time
Approach
Activity-based costing driven by measured effort, then margin decomposition by customer, site, and SKU profile
You get
A margin waterfall per account showing exactly which activities consume it — the evidence base for renewal and rate conversations
Predictive Commercial

Churn & volume-decline early warning

Accounts rarely leave suddenly. Volume tapers, order patterns change, and the signals are spread across months of transactions nobody reads end to end.

Data
Volume and order-frequency trends, service performance history, claims and complaints, contract terms and renewal dates
Approach
Change-point detection on account trajectories plus a classification model for attrition risk
You get
A monthly watch list for account managers, ordered by revenue at risk, with the specific signals that triggered each flag
Prescriptive Pricing

Quote & rate decision support

New business is priced from comparable deals and instinct. Two accounts with similar pallet counts can impose very different demands on the building.

Data
Historical quotes and outcomes, realized cost to serve by account profile, site capacity and constraints, seasonality of the incoming volume
Approach
Predicted cost to serve for the proposed profile, combined with margin scenarios across a rate range
You get
A pre-quote profitability estimate with sensitivity to volume and mix assumptions, produced in minutes rather than days

05 — Working together

Bringing us a problem

You do not need to know what model to use, or whether the data exists. You need to know what decision is hard. Bring the problem in this shape and we can usually tell you within a week whether it's tractable.

A good problem statement

  • The decision What is being decided, by whom, and how often?
  • Today's method How is it decided now — a report, experience, a spreadsheet?
  • The cost of being wrong What happens when the current approach misses? Overtime, detention, spoilage, a lost account?
  • The desired timing When would the answer need to arrive to be useful?
  • Available data Which systems touch this process? A rough list is enough.
  • Success What would you expect to see change if this worked?

What good candidates look like

  • The decision repeats — daily, weekly, per load, per SKU.
  • The outcome is recorded somewhere, so we can learn from history.
  • Several factors interact, which is why a single report hasn't solved it.
  • Someone owns the decision and is willing to change how they make it.

Signs it's not ready yet

  • The underlying process isn't documented or varies by site with no record of the difference.
  • The data is captured on paper or in free-text notes only.
  • It's a one-time question — a good analysis answers it faster than a model would.
  • The real blocker is a policy or system change, not a lack of insight.
Week 0

Intake conversation. We restate the problem as a decision and confirm it's worth solving.

Weeks 1–2

Feasibility. Do the data and the signal exist? A short, honest go / no-go.

Weeks 3–8

Prototype. A working model reviewed with the business expert, tested against history.

Ongoing

Pilot at one site, then productionize, monitor, and expand where it holds up.

06 — Reference

Plain-language glossary

The vocabulary you'll hear in a project kickoff, without the jargon tax.

Model

A rule learned from historical data that turns inputs into an output — for example, turning "customer, day of week, season, appointment count" into "expected pallets received."

Feature

An input to a model. Day of week, ambient temperature, SKU velocity, and customer tenure are all features. Choosing good features is usually where domain knowledge pays off most.

Training & validation

Training is the model learning from past data. Validation is testing it on data it never saw, to estimate how it will perform on next week's real decisions rather than on the past it memorized.

Machine learning

A family of methods that learn patterns from data instead of being programmed with explicit rules. It is a subset of data science, not a synonym for it.

Prediction interval

The honest range around a forecast. "1,240 pallets" is a guess; "most likely 1,240, and 90% of the time between 1,050 and 1,480" is a plan you can staff to.

Optimization

Finding the best option under constraints — the lowest-cost load plan that still honors delivery windows, temperature requirements, and equipment limits.

Anomaly detection

Learning what "normal" looks like so that unusual behavior — a compressor drawing more power than its own history predicts — surfaces automatically.

Model drift

Performance decaying as the business changes. New customers, new SKUs, and new sites all shift the patterns a model learned, which is why monitoring and retraining are part of the deliverable.

Data pipeline

The automated path data takes from source systems to a form the model can use. Most of the effort in a project lives here, and it's what makes results repeatable instead of one-off.

Ground truth

The recorded, verified outcome we compare predictions against. Without it, a model can't be trained or scored — which is why capturing outcomes is worth the effort even before a project starts.

Have a decision that's hard to make well?

Bring it to the data science team. The first conversation costs nothing but half an hour, and the answer is sometimes "here's a report that solves it today."
